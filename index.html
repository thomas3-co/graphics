Creating a 3D Mockup and Composition

What: A 3D mockup is a digital visual showing your design applied on a realistic object like a phone, tablet, or poster. It helps visualize how your work looks in real life.

Why: It gives your design depth and realism. 3D mockups make presentations professional, help with perspective accuracy, and make your work more engaging.

When: After completing your main design (photo or layout), use Photoshop or an online tool like Photopea or Smartmockups to place your design inside a 3D frame or screen.

How:

  • Prepare your final image or layout.
  • Use a smart object or mockup template.
  • Insert your design into the mockup layer.
  • Adjust lighting, reflection, and perspective for realism.
  • Align background color or blur to match your theme.
  • Export the final composition as JPG or PNG.

The New Project Menu

Before you can design anything, you need a canvas. The New Project menu is split into three panels working together: a filter on the left, ready-made presets in the middle, and a fully manual setup on the right.

1 · The Left Panel — Template Filtering & Search

Think of this panel as a smart shopping filter in a pre-made design store. Instead of building a painting from scratch, you're browsing pre-drawn outline kits.

  1. With Symbols (checkbox) — toggles whether templates containing vector graphics, logos, and line-art shapes appear in the results.
  2. With Photo (checkbox) — filters templates to include or exclude real stock photographs.
  3. Keywords (search bar & refresh ↻) — filters the community library by subject tag (e.g. typing "Fitness" or "Real Estate").
Analogies: "With Symbols" is like checking a box at a craft store that says "show me kits with pre-cut wooden shapes and stickers." "With Photo" is like asking for kits that come with real photos printed in the background. "Keywords" is like asking a store clerk to take you straight to the aisle with restaurant menu templates.
2 · The Middle Panel — Preset Categories & Canvas Sizes

This panel is a pre-cut canvas rack. Instead of measuring and cutting your own wooden board, you grab a frame already built for a specific job.

The categories (top tabs):

  • Social — standard dimensions tailored to fit social media feeds without awkward cropping.
  • Print — standard physical paper sizes (A4, Letter), automatically configured for ink and paper.
  • Photo — sizes matching physical picture frames (4×6", 5×7", 8×10").
  • Screen — digital display standards matching TVs, monitors, and laptops (1080p, 4K).
  • Mobile — dimensions tailored to phone-screen proportions, for UI mockups or wallpapers.
  • Ads — standard digital ad-banner dimensions recognized by web networks.
  • 2ⁿ (Power of Two) — sizes formatted for 3D textures (256×256, 512×512, 1024×1024, 2048×2048).

Individual presets under Social:

  • FB Page Cover (1640 × 664 px) — wide rectangle sized for Facebook business header banners.
  • FB Event Image (1920 × 1080 px) — standard 16:9 landscape layout for event invitations.
  • FB Group Header (1640 × 856 px) — banner scaled specifically for Facebook group pages.
  • Instagram (1080 × 1080 px) — classic 1:1 square canvas.
  • Insta Story (1080 × 1920 px) — full-screen vertical 9:16 layout for mobile phones.
  • Insta Portrait (1080 × 1350 px) — vertical 4:5 ratio, a tall poster shape that fills more of the feed.
  • YouTube Thumbnail (1280 × 720 px) — standard 16:9 widescreen video preview image.
  • YouTube Profile (800 × 800 px) — high-res square avatar; YouTube crops it into a circle automatically.
  • YouTube Cover (2560 × 1440 px) — large canvas required to stay sharp across mobile, desktop, and TV apps.
  • Twitter Profile (400 × 400 px) — standard profile-picture square for X/Twitter.
  • Twitter Header (1500 × 500 px) — ultra-wide 3:1 banner for profile headers.
Analogies: Instagram's square canvas is a standard ceramic tile — equal on all sides in a grid. Insta Story is like turning your phone vertical to fill every millimeter of the display. Insta Portrait is a tall poster that takes up maximum vertical space so people spend more time scrolling past it.
3 · The Right Panel — Manual Configuration & Technical Parameters

This is your custom carpenter workstation — where you measure and configure a raw canvas from scratch.

  1. Name (New Project) — gives your project file a default title before you start working, like labeling a folder or writing a title on the back of a canvas.
  2. Width & Height (e.g. 1280 × 720) — defines the horizontal and vertical boundary of your working space, like measuring a wooden frame with a tape measure.
  3. Swap button (⇄) — flips the width and height values instantly, like rotating a rectangular frame 90° to switch between Landscape and Portrait.
  4. Units dropdown — sets what system the Width/Height numbers use: Pixels (best for anything viewed on a screen), Inches / Centimeters / Millimeters (physical measurements, best for print), or Percent (relative proportions based on another image's size).
  5. DPI & Resolution Unit — Dots Per Inch controls pixel density: 72 DPI is standard for computer screens; 300 DPI is standard for physical ink printing.
  6. Background — the starting color of your base layer: White/Black starts with a solid painted base; Transparent starts with an empty grid (ideal for logos and stickers); Custom lets you pick any starting color.
  7. Artboards (checkbox) — lets multiple independent canvases live side-by-side in the same file, like a long worktable with several sketchpads laid out so you can design a multi-card set all at once.
  8. Mode — RGB is the red/green/blue light mixing used by screens; 8-bit gives 256 brightness levels per channel (16.7 million total colors), while 16-bit/32-bit are used in high-end photography to prevent color banding in gradients like skies.
  9. Profile (sRGB) — the standard color map used across the web, so the same blue looks the same on an iPhone, a Samsung phone, and a PC monitor.
  10. Create button — builds the file with your chosen specifications and opens the editor.
Tip: DPI is like thread count on bed sheets — 72 DPI is coarse burlap, fine for a screen viewed from a distance; 300 DPI is fine silk, holding crisp detail up close on printed paper.
Visual Example: Bit depth is how many distinct paint tubes you keep in your art box — 8-bit gives you a standard set of paints, while 16-bit/32-bit gives you hundreds of microscopic shade variations of the same color.

Introduction to Graphic Design & Photoshop

Graphic Design communicates ideas visually using typography, images, colors, and layout. Photoshop is a digital workspace where imagination becomes tangible.

  1. Open Photoshop and explore the workspace: Menu Bar, Toolbar, Panels, Options Bar.
  2. Familiarize yourself with essential panels: Layers, Adjustments, Properties, History.
  3. Learn non-destructive editing: use Layer Masks, Smart Objects, and Adjustment Layers.
  4. Practice: Draw simple shapes and apply color fills to understand layer stacking.
Analogy: Photoshop is like a designer's kitchen: brushes are spices, layers are ingredients, masks are containers, and smart objects are reusable recipes.
Tip: Always keep a "backup layer" for original images to prevent accidental mistakes.
Visual Example: Imagine a blank canvas as a plate. Each layer is a separate ingredient. Masks are lids that cover parts of layers without deleting them. Smart Objects are frozen ingredients you can use later without changing the original taste.

How to Open, Save and Export Files in Photoshop

Save and export is the loop every Photoshop project runs on: open a file, work on it in layers, save the layered version, then export a flat copy for wherever it is going. Skip the middle step and you can never revise the edit; skip the last one and you have a file nobody else can open.

Photoshop's file handling trips people up more often than any of its tools, and always in the same way: the finished JPEG exists, the layered version does not, and the only way to change anything is to start over.

The habit that prevents it: Open the file, save a layered PSD early, keep working, and export a flat copy only at the very end. Day 2 runs that loop end to end on one photograph, adding a sun flare and a set of light rays along the way so there is something worth saving.
Part 1 · How to Open Files in Photopea

Photopea is a browser-based graphic editor that supports multiple file formats (.PSD, .AI, .XCF, .PNG, .JPG, .SVG, etc.).

1. File Menu (Standard Local Import)
How to do it: Go to File → Open (Ctrl+O / Cmd+O), select your image, and open it.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 1.0 (1990).
How it worked before & why: Before digital image editors allowed opening images through a graphical file menu, image data had to be read from dedicated hardware scanners, system frame buffers, or raw bit-mapped files using specialized command-line scripts. Standard local file import gave non-programmer designers a standard desktop interface for opening files from hard drives or floppy disks.
2. Drag & Drop
How to do it: Drag a file from your file manager directly onto the canvas area to load it.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 7.0 (2002) for basic canvas loading, expanded in Photoshop CS2 (2005) to auto-generate Smart Objects on drag-and-drop.
How it worked before & why: Before OS-level drag-and-drop, users had to use File → Place or File → Open, manually select the open canvas (Ctrl+A), and paste it (Ctrl+V) into their target canvas. Drag-and-drop sped up multi-file workflows by skipping the manual import menus and repetitive copy-paste.
3. Open from URL
How to do it: Go to File → Open More → Open from URL and paste an image link directly.
Photoshop version: Photoshop CC (2013), integrating web and Adobe Stock asset fetching.
How it worked before & why: Before web-fetching, users had to right-click an image in a browser, "Save Image As...", save it locally, then open the downloaded file in Photoshop. Web URL fetching removed the local-storage clutter and let people edit straight from a web link.
4. Clipboard (Paste Direct)
How to do it: Copy an image to your clipboard (Ctrl+C) and paste it into Photopea (Ctrl+V). File → New also auto-detects clipboard dimensions.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 2.0 (1991).
How it worked before & why: Early editors could only open files already saved to disk — a screen snippet or copied frame had to be saved as a temporary file first. Native clipboard ingestion let visual data move instantly between applications.
5. Open from Cloud / Remote Storage
How to do it: Go to File → Open More to connect directly to Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, or Google Photos.
Photoshop version: Photoshop CC 2014 (v15.0), with Creative Cloud asset integration.
How it worked before & why: Users previously needed local desktop sync utilities to pull cloud files down before opening them. Native cloud integration supports remote, collaborative workflows across multiple devices without manual downloads.
Part 2 · How to Save and Export in Photopea

Photopea distinguishes between saving working source project files and exporting final rendered output images.

1. Saving editable source files

Save as PSD (recommended)
How to do it: File → Save as PSD (Ctrl+S / Cmd+S).
Photoshop version: Photoshop 3.0 (1994).
How it worked before & why: Before 3.0, programs saved images as single flat files — text typed over a photo and saved burned permanently into the pixels. The PSD format arrived alongside layers to save structural metadata, masks, text paths, and separate layers without flattening them.
Save to Cloud Storage
How to do it: File → Save More → Save to Google Drive / Dropbox / OneDrive.
Photoshop version: Photoshop CC (2013).
How it worked before & why: Users had to save locally, then manually upload the PSD to a server through a browser. Direct cloud saving streamlined online backups and multi-device access.

2. Exporting rendered output files

PNG — lossless compression with transparency support.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 5.5 (1999), via "Save for Web".
How it worked before & why: Web designers relied on GIF for transparent graphics, limited to 256 colors. PNG added 24-bit full color with smooth alpha-channel transparency and no quality loss.
JPG / JPEG — lossy compression for small file sizes; no transparency.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 2.5 (1992).
How it worked before & why: Uncompressed raster files (TIFF, BMP) were massive and hard to store or transfer over early networks. JPEG compression drastically shrank photo file sizes.
SVG — scalable vector format.
Photoshop version: Photoshop CC 2015.
How it worked before & why: Raster graphics pixelate when enlarged, so designers had to hop over to a vector app like Illustrator. SVG export brought resolution-independent output directly into a photo editor for responsive web design.
PDF — document and print format.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 6.0 (2000).
How it worked before & why: Sending artwork to commercial printers meant manually placing exported images into desktop-publishing software like QuarkXPress or PageMaker. Direct PDF export delivers print-ready files with embedded fonts, vectors, and color profiles from the image editor itself.
GIF — frame animations and limited-palette images.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 5.5 (1999).
How it worked before & why: Early editors targeted static print or display images only. GIF export arrived during the dot-com era so designers could build light web banners and animations.
Part 3 · Understanding Layers — The Glass Sheet Analogy
Think of layers as a vertical stack of transparent sheets of glass resting directly over one another.
  • Transparency: an empty layer is a clean, transparent pane — you see straight through to the panes below.
  • Painting / content: a red dot painted on the top sheet covers whatever sits behind it; the clear areas around it still show the lower sheets.
  • Stacking order: the top-most layer in the Layers Panel is the front-most sheet, closest to your eye — the bottom pane is the Background.
  • Isolation: modifying one sheet leaves the others completely untouched — you can reorder or hide any sheet without altering the rest.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 3.0 (1994).
How it worked before & why: Prior to 1994, image editors worked like a single piece of paper — text painted across a photo destroyed the pixels underneath permanently, and fixing a typo meant repainting the background by hand or starting over. Co-founder Thomas Knoll invented layers so elements (text, subjects, backgrounds) could sit independently on top of each other, turning image editing from destructive paint-over work into flexible compositing.

Part 5 · Pixels and Resolution Explained
  • Pixel (Picture Element): the smallest individual square block of colored light that forms a digital raster image.
  • Grid structure: every raster image sits on a 2D grid where each pixel holds a specific RGB color value.
  • Pixel density & scaling: zooming in close enough shows the individual colored squares — a state called pixelation.
  • Resolution (PPI/DPI): Pixels Per Inch. Standard web displays use 72 PPI; high-quality printing needs 300 DPI to keep pixels from looking blocky.
Photoshop version: Photoshop 1.0 (1990) for the base pixel grid engine, and Photoshop 6.0 (2000) for resolution-independent vector shapes layered over pixel grids.
How it worked before & why: Early monitors could only render basic monochrome or fixed-resolution low-color images. Photoshop was built to process multi-channel RGB pixel data so desktop computers could edit photography tone-by-tone and pixel-by-pixel — vector layers were later combined with pixel grids in Photoshop 6.0 so smooth vector graphics and pixel-based photography could share one canvas.

Raster vs Vector Graphics

Raster images are pixel-based, perfect for detailed photos. Vector graphics are path-based and scalable without losing quality.

  • Raster: Photos, textures, paintings.
  • Vector: Logos, icons, illustrations requiring scalability.
  1. Open a raster image, zoom in — notice pixels when enlarged.
  2. Draw a shape with the Pen Tool — crisp at any zoom.
  3. Save raster as JPG/PNG, vector as SVG/AI.
  4. Practice: Trace a raster logo into a vector path.
Pen Tool: Precise path creation.
Analogy: Tracing a logo on tracing paper for clean outlines.
Pro Tip: Combine raster & vector: vector logos with raster textures for realism.
Visual Example: Imagine a photo of a cat (raster) vs a cartoon cat outline (vector). Zoom the photo: pixels appear. Zoom the cartoon: it stays sharp, clean, and scalable.

Photopea Overview

Photopea is an online Photoshop alternative supporting layers, masks, smart objects, and adjustments.

  1. Visit photopea.com.
  2. Open or drag an image.
  3. Use Move, Brush, Clone Stamp, and Adjustment Layers.
  4. Save as PSD for future edits or export as PNG/JPG.
  5. Practice: Open a Photoshop PSD in Photopea and observe layer compatibility.
Analogy: Photopea is a pocket Photoshop — light and ready anywhere.
Tip: Learn Photopea shortcuts; most match Photoshop for faster workflow.
Visual Example: Picture carrying a small, foldable art studio in your laptop browser, with all brushes, layers, and panels ready.

Removing Backgrounds and Subjects

Essential for product design, e-commerce, social media, and composites.

  1. Select the subject using Quick Selection Tool or Select Subject.
  2. Refine edges using Select and Mask.
  3. Apply Layer Mask to hide the background non-destructively.
  4. Use Refine Edge Brush for hair or intricate details.
Analogy: Digital scissors that are reversible.
Tip: Check edges at 200% zoom for perfection.
Visual Example: Imagine cutting a portrait out of a magazine but having invisible scissors that allow you to adjust edges after cutting.

Passport Size Photo

Precise cropping, clean background, and correct exposure are key.

  1. Crop to 2x2 inches using Crop Tool.
  2. Adjust background with Solid Color Fill or Brush Tool.
  3. Dodge & Burn to balance skin tone.
  4. Save at 300 dpi.
Analogy: Trimming a printed photo to fit exactly into a passport window.
Tip: Use soft brush edges for smooth background transitions.
Visual Example: Picture a perfectly framed passport headshot on a white canvas, with soft shadows blending into the background.

Face and Skin Retouching

Enhance portraits subtly while preserving texture.

  1. Duplicate the original layer.
  2. Spot Healing Brush for blemishes.
  3. Frequency Separation: separate color & texture for precise edits.
  4. Dodge & Burn for highlights and contours.
Healing Brush: Digital makeup without destroying skin texture.
Tip: Zoom at 100–200% to see true texture.
Visual Example: Imagine painting small corrections over a face while keeping pores and skin details intact.

Teeth and Eye Whitening, Reflection & Correction

Brighten eyes and teeth subtly while maintaining realism.

  1. Dodge Tool 10–15% exposure for brightening.
  2. Add reflections with Overlay layers.
  3. Liquify Tool for small alignment fixes.
Liquify Tool: Gentle reshaping like soft clay.
Tip: Keep whitening subtle to avoid a fake look.
Visual Example: Visualize adding a soft highlight to the eyes like a sunbeam, without changing natural color.

Posture Correction & Dress Mockups

Adjust posture and garments for realistic presentation using Smart Objects.

  1. Puppet Warp for subtle posture corrections.
  2. Convert garments to Smart Objects.
  3. Warp Transform for folds and fabric flow.
  4. Adjust shadows and highlights for realism.
Puppet Warp: Controlled string movement like a puppet.
Tip: Make small incremental adjustments to avoid unnatural distortion.
Visual Example: Picture moving an arm slightly, like bending a paper doll, and the clothes following naturally with realistic folds.

Tool Reuse & Project Application

Skills compound — mastering one tool allows multiple creative applications.

  • Healing Brush: Polishing portraits & products.
  • Pen Tool: Clean logos & scalable shapes.
  • Liquify: Fashion edits & symmetry correction.
  • Smart Objects: Reusable templates, non-destructive scaling.
Tip: Always name layers & groups. Reusable workflows depend on organization.
Visual Example: Imagine a toolbox where each tool can be picked and reused in multiple projects — nothing is wasted, everything is reusable.
