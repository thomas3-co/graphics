Week 1 — Graphic Design Software & Foundations

Day 1: Graphic Design Software

A tour of the apps designers use — Photoshop, Illustrator, Photopea, Canva and more.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Photoshop — Adobe's industry-standard raster photo-editing software., Illustrator — Adobe's vector-based illustration software., Photopea — A free, browser-based editor compatible with Photoshop files — no install needed., Canva — A beginner-friendly, drag-and-drop design tool for quick social/print graphics., InDesign — Adobe's page-layout software for multi-page documents like magazines and books.

Different software suits different jobs: raster editors for photos, vector tools for logos and scalable art, layout tools for multi-page documents. This class uses Photopea, a free browser-based Photoshop alternative.

Day 2: What is Graphic Design?

The elements and principles that make visual communication actually work.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Graphic Design — Visual communication that uses images, typography and layout to convey a message., Typography — The art of arranging type so it is readable and visually appealing., Layout — How elements are arranged and positioned on a page or screen., Balance — How visual weight is distributed across a design., Contrast — Making one element stand out by making it different from the rest.

Good graphic design isn't decoration — it's communication. Every color, font, and placement choice should serve the message you're trying to send.

Week 2 — Photopea, Pixels & Layers

Day 3: Photopea Interface, Pixels & Raster

Tour the Photopea workspace, then learn what pixels and raster images actually are.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Workspace — The arrangement of menus, panels and tools you work in., Panels — Dockable windows like Layers, History and Properties., Pixel — The smallest square of colored light that makes up a raster image., Raster — Images made of a grid of pixels — detailed, but not infinitely scalable., Vector — Graphics built from math-based paths — scalable to any size without blurring.

Photopea's layout mirrors Photoshop's, so everything you learn here transfers directly. Knowing pixel vs. raster vs. vector is the foundation every later editing decision builds on.

Day 4: Destructive vs. Non-Destructive Editing & Layers

Why layers matter, and how to edit without ever harming your original image.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Layer — Photoshop's building blocks — stacked, independent pieces of an image., Destructive Editing — Editing that permanently changes the original pixels., Non-Destructive Editing — Editing that preserves the original image and can be undone or adjusted later., Group — Organize multiple layers together into one folder., Opacity — Adjust how visible a layer is, from fully hidden to fully shown.

Layers let you edit non-linearly — nothing is permanent until you flatten the file. Always prefer non-destructive techniques so you can change your mind later without starting over.

Week 3 — Around the World with a Twist (Project Week)

Day 5: Removing Backgrounds

Remove the original background and prepare a clean subject edge for the new composition.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Select Subject / Background Remover — Automatically detects and separates the subject from its background., Smudge Tool — Pushes and blends pixels like wet paint — great for softening rough cutout edges., Fringe / White Line — A thin leftover strip of the old background color left behind around a cutout., Defringe / Contract — Shrinks a selection edge inward slightly to remove leftover background pixels., Layer Mask — Hides parts of a layer non-destructively so the cutout stays fully reversible.

Even a clean selection often leaves a faint white line around the edges. Zoom in to 200%+ and use the Smudge Tool (or Defringe/Contract) to blend that line away so the cutout sits seamlessly against its new background.

Day 6: Smudge Tool + Minimum + Gaussian Blur

Use the Smudge Tool to refine edges, Filter → Other → Minimum to remove a white fringe, then place the subject into the world composition and use Gaussian Blur for realistic depth.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Background Removal — Separates the main subject cleanly from the original background., Compositing — Moves a cut-out subject into a new image or environment and blends it with the scene., Gaussian Blur — A soft blur that reduces detail and simulates depth or distance., Depth of Field — The visual relationship between foreground, subject and background elements in a composite., Layer Mask — Adjusts the edges and mask of a cut-out so the subject blends naturally into its new environment.

A convincing composite depends on more than a clean cut-out. Match scale, lighting, color and sharpness, then apply Gaussian Blur selectively so distant parts of the scene feel farther away and the subject belongs in the new world.
Week 3 project work: remove the background, place the subject into the Around the World scene, and use Gaussian Blur where needed to create realistic depth.

Week 4 — Advanced Portrait Editing

Day 1: Creating a Passport Size Photo

Crop, retouch and format a professional passport-size photo from start to finish.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Crop Tool — Trims the canvas down to an exact size and ratio., Passport Size (2x2 in) — The standard 2x2 inch dimension required for most ID and passport photos., Solid Color Fill — Replaces the background with a flat, regulation color — usually plain white., Dodge & Burn — Lightens or darkens areas to even out skin tone and lighting., Resolution (DPI) — Passport photos are typically saved at 300 DPI for sharp, print-quality results.

Passport photos follow strict formatting rules — exact size, plain background, even lighting, natural expression — so precision matters more here than creativity.

Day 1: Text on Any Surface (Vanishing Point)

Apply realistic text on walls, floors, or objects using the Vanishing Point filter.

Tap to flip

Objective: Learn to map and blend text realistically to perspective planes.

  • Open your background image and create a new layer for text.
  • Go to Filter → Vanishing Point and define perspective grids.
  • Paste or drag your text layer into the vanishing point plane.
  • Adjust lighting, opacity, and blending mode (Multiply or Overlay).
PROJECT DAY

Day 2: Refine Vanishing Point Text & 3D Pop-Out Effect

Refine lighting and texture, then create an illusion of objects or text popping out of the frame.

Tap to flip

Objective: Refine realism from Day 1, then combine layer masking and 3D perspective to make subjects break out of the photo boundaries.

Creating a 3D Mockup and Composition

What: A 3D mockup is a digital visual showing your design applied on a realistic object like a phone, tablet, or poster. It helps visualize how your work looks in real life.

Read Me — Course Notes

Three focused weekly note packs. Open a card to study the lesson, review the vocabulary, use the tips, then choose Mark as Read when you are ready for the 7-question check.

WEEK 1 Open · Save · Export

Week 1 builds the file-handling habits you need before editing: create or open the correct document, save an editable working file, and export a finished copy in the format your project requires.

01

How to Open

Use File → Open to bring an existing image or Photoshop document into the workspace. You can also open a file from the start screen or drag a supported file into the editor.

  1. Choose the file you actually intend to edit.
  2. Check the image dimensions and resolution before doing detailed work.
  3. Confirm that the document opens with the expected layers or image content.
02

How to Save

Save protects your editable working file. For Photoshop projects, save the working version as a PSD so layers and editability remain available.

  1. Use File → Save As when creating the first named working copy.
  2. Choose a clear filename and a folder you can find again.
  3. Save repeatedly while working so your latest edits are preserved.
03

How to Export

Export creates a delivery copy for a specific use. Use File → Export → Export As when you need a JPG, PNG, or another final image format.

  1. Keep the PSD as the editable master.
  2. Choose the format that matches the destination.
  3. Check dimensions, transparency, quality, and filename before confirming the export.
Week 1 Vocabulary

PSD — An editable Photoshop document that preserves layers and other working information.
Open — Opening an existing file so it becomes the document you can edit.
Save — Writing the current working document to a saved file so the editable work is preserved.
Export — Creating a final-use copy of the design in a selected output format such as JPG or PNG.
Folder — The physical or digital location where the working file is stored.

Quick tip: Think PSD = master working file and export = delivery copy. Do not replace your editable master with a flattened delivery image.

WEEK 2 Destructive vs. Non-Destructive Editing

Week 2 explains what happens when an edit changes the original pixels directly, why that can become difficult to undo later, and how a non-destructive workflow keeps the original information available.

A

Destructive Editing

A destructive edit permanently changes the pixels or content you are working on. If you later need the original detail, you may have to undo many steps or reopen an earlier version.

Example: painting directly on the only copy of a raster layer and then saving over the original can remove information from that working layer.
B

Non-Destructive Editing

A non-destructive workflow keeps the original information available while edits are applied through layers, masks, adjustment layers, smart objects, or other reversible methods.

Goal: make an edit, inspect the result, and still be able to change or remove that edit without rebuilding the entire image.
C

Raster Images & Pixels

A raster image is built from a grid of tiny picture elements called pixels. Each pixel stores color information that contributes to the complete photograph or graphic.

Important: when a raster image is enlarged too far, individual pixels become more visible and the image can lose apparent sharpness.
Week 2 Vocabulary

Destructive Editing — An edit that directly changes original image information or pixels and can be difficult to reverse later.
Non-Destructive Editing — An editing workflow that preserves original information so changes can be adjusted or removed later.
Raster Image — A digital image made from a grid of colored picture elements rather than mathematical paths.
Pixel — A tiny picture element in a raster image that stores color information.
Reversible Workflow — A way of applying an edit while keeping the source information available for later changes.

Why it matters: non-destructive editing gives you more control. You can compare versions, correct mistakes, change your mind, and keep the original image information available.

WEEK 3 Remove · Refine · Place · Blur

Week 3 turns background removal into a complete composition workflow: isolate the subject, clean the edge, remove a white fringe, place the subject into the world scene, and create realistic depth with Gaussian Blur.

01

Removing Backgrounds

Use a selection method that fits the subject, create a clean subject mask or isolated layer, and inspect difficult edges such as hair, fur, and fine clothing details.

02

Smudge Tool

Use the Smudge Tool carefully around fine edges to blend or restore small areas. Keep the brush controlled and avoid smearing important facial or clothing details.

03

Removing the White Fringe

If the old background leaves a bright halo around the subject, refine the edge instead of accepting the visible outline. A white fringe is especially obvious when the subject is placed on a darker or colorful background.

04

Filter → Other → Minimum

With the subject selection or mask targeted, use Filter → Other → Minimum to contract the edge slightly. This can pull the mask inward and help remove a thin white line left by the original background.

05

Place the Subject into the World

Move the cleaned subject into the new world composition. Match scale, position, lighting, color, and perspective so the subject feels like part of the scene rather than a pasted cutout.

06

Gaussian Blur for Realistic Depth

Use Gaussian Blur selectively to create depth of field. Background elements that sit farther from the camera can be softened more than the main subject, helping the composition feel photographic.

Week 3 Vocabulary

Background Removal — The process of isolating a subject from the original background so it can be placed into another composition.
Smudge Tool — A Photoshop tool that blends pixels by dragging color and texture from one area into another.
White Fringe — A visible light-colored outline left around a subject after the old background has been removed.
Minimum — A Photoshop filter found under Filter → Other that contracts an edge by a chosen amount.
Gaussian Blur — A blur filter used to soften image detail and simulate reduced focus or depth of field.
World Composition — The visual arrangement where the isolated subject is integrated into a new scene or environment.
Subject Edge — A controlled layer or selection edge that separates the subject from its previous background.

Pro tip: clean the edge before adding blur. Gaussian Blur should help establish depth in the final scene, not hide a poorly removed background.
